2. Six words you need before anything else

You cannot follow the problem without these. Each one has a tiny example so it sticks.

1. Revenue — money coming IN from sales

You run a juice stall. You sell 100 glasses at ₹10 each. Your revenue is ₹1,000. Notice this says nothing about whether you made money.

2. Profit — what is LEFT after you pay for everything

Those 100 glasses cost you ₹900 in oranges, ice and cups. Revenue ₹1,000, costs ₹900, so your profit is ₹100.

3. Margin — profit as a percentage of revenue

₹100 profit on ₹1,000 revenue = a 10% margin. Now imagine you only kept ₹0.20 out of that ₹1,000. That is a 0.02% margin — and that is the number this whole case turns on. Remember it.

4. Subsidiary — a company owned by another company

Your juice stall company buys an orange farm. The farm is now your subsidiary. You own it.

5. Standalone vs consolidated — the single shop, or the whole family

Standalone = just the juice stall's own numbers. Consolidated = the juice stall plus the orange farm plus anything else you own, all added into one big number.

6. Audited — checked by an outsider

Anyone can type a number into a spreadsheet. Audited means an independent accounting firm examined the records and signed their name to say the numbers are real. Audited numbers can be trusted. Unaudited numbers are just a claim.

If you only remember one thing from this page

A company can have enormous revenue and almost no profit at the same time. That is not automatically suspicious — and understanding why is the whole puzzle.

3. The problem we were given

You are given a role to play. You are an analyst at an investment fund. The fund owns shares in a gold company. Something has gone wrong, and your boss wants your recommendation today.

Who you areA research analyst at Aarohan Capital, a made-up investment fund that manages ₹4,500 crore of other people's money.
What you ownA small amount of shares in Rajesh Exports, a gold company.
What went wrongThe government's market regulator says it is not satisfied the company's sales figures are real, and has started an investigation.
Your jobTell your boss what to do with the shares, and explain why.

Now here is the actual problem, using a shop

Forget gold for a minute. Imagine a corner shop.

So you say: fine — then show me your sales book, your supplier bills, and your bank statements.

And the shop does not show you.

That is the entire case

A huge claimed number. A tiny checkable number. A reasonable explanation for the gap. And then no paperwork to back the explanation up. Everything from here is just this same shape, with much bigger numbers.

4. The company, and why its shape matters

Rajesh Exports is an Indian company that deals in gold. But it owns other companies in other countries, and that is where nearly all the money supposedly is.

How lopsided is it?

In the most recent year the group said it took in ₹4,23,099 crore. Of that, only ₹7,027 crore came from the Indian company that Indian rules let us inspect. Everything else — 98.34% — came from the overseas companies.

So when someone asks “can we trust this company's numbers?”, they are really asking about that big gold block — the part nobody outside the company has ever been allowed to look at.

5. The argument, from both sides

Before our answer, you need to understand why this is genuinely hard — and why the company's defence is not stupid.

The company's defence (and it is a good one)

Gold refining works differently from a normal business. A refinery takes in gold, melts it, purifies it, and sells it on. The gold is worth a fortune, but the refinery only keeps a tiny fee.

Think of a money-changer at the airport

Someone hands over ₹1,00,000 to change into dollars. The money-changer keeps ₹200 as a fee. Did the money-changer “earn” ₹1,00,000 or ₹200? Depending on how you write the books, both answers can be correct. That is exactly the dispute here.

So the company says: the regulator is comparing our fee figure with our total value passing through figure. Of course one is 500 times the other. Nothing is missing.

This could be true. Our answer says so openly. A good argument has to beat the other side at its strongest, not at its weakest.

Why we did not accept it — three reasons

Reason 1 — the two numbers move in opposite directions

If the big number really comes from the refinery's work, then when the refinery gets busier, both numbers should rise together. They do not.

The fee explanation can make one number bigger than the other. It cannot make them travel in opposite directions, because a fee is always a slice of the total. If the total grew, the fee should have grown too.

The fair objection — and our answer

Someone sharp will say: the top line covers ALL the overseas companies, the bottom line covers only Valcambi. Maybe the work just moved to a different company in the group. That is a fair point and we say so in the memo.

But it costs them. If the work moved, it moved to companies that have no gold refinery at all — which makes things worse, not better. And it would be settled in one page: a simple breakdown of which company earned what. That breakdown has never been produced.

Reason 2 — they sold more but earned almost nothing

A real trading business earns a thin but steady slice. Sell twice as much, earn roughly twice as much. Here, sales went up and profit nearly vanished.

There is a sharper version. Compare the part we can inspect with the part we cannot:

In the most recent yearRevenueProfitMargin
The Indian company — audited, inspectable ₹7,027 cr₹24 cr0.34%
The overseas companies — nobody can inspect ₹4,16,072 cr₹71 cr0.017%

The visible part earns twenty times better than the invisible part. The bit we can check behaves like a normal business. The bit we cannot check does not.

The fair objection — and our answer

Gold prices swing, so maybe they lost money on stock they were holding, or on a bad bet, or took a one-off write-off. All possible. But each of those would show up as one bad year, a single visible hit — not as a smooth two-year slide from 0.42% to 0.12% to 0.02%. And none has been disclosed.

Reason 3 — gold that heavy cannot move quietly

This is our strongest point and it works backwards from what you would expect. Read it twice.

Why this is clever, in one sentence

We do not say the amount is too big to be real — it isn't, and saying so would be easy to attack. We say the opposite: if it is real, then mountains of paperwork must exist, much of it held by other companies in other countries. So the missing paperwork is more damning, not less.

The fair objection — and our answer

“Those records are confidential, they name our customers.” True, but it does not work. A reconciliation statement and an auditor's sign-off name nobody at all, and documents can be handed to a regulator privately, under seal. Confidentiality explains why records are not published. It does not explain why they were not handed over.

6. What we decided

There were four allowed answers. We had to pick exactly one.

The reasoning, in four steps

Why not just wait and see? (This is the answer most teams will give)

Because waiting is not free. If the investigation ends badly, the share price collapses and there is nobody left to buy from you. You would be stuck holding it. So waiting gives you all of the danger and none of the ability to escape. It only looks like the safe choice.

One thing we are careful NOT to say

We never accuse anyone of fraud. The problem asks you to think like an analyst, not a detective. Our answer is narrower and much harder to argue with: we cannot measure this, so we should not own it — and here are the exact six documents that would change our mind.
